The transitions between these underlying states follow a first-order Markov chain, defined rigorously by a transition probability matrix $P$:$$P_{ij} = \mathbb{P}(S_t = j \mid S_{t-1} = i)$$For a standard two-state model identifying "Bull/Trend" ($S_t = 1$) and "Bear/Mean-Reversion" ($S_t = 2$), the asset returns $r_t$ can be modeled as Gaussian emissions completely dependent on the current state:$$r_t \mid S_t = k \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu_k, \sigma_k^2)$$In a persistent, high-stress bear market, such as the 2022 sample period, the transition probabilities $P_{22}$ remain elevated, leading to a prolonged regime characterized by negative drift ($\mu_2 < 0$) and substantially elevated variance ($\sigma_2^2 > \sigma_1^2$). Ascending channel breakout strategies inherently assume a low-variance, positive-drift environment (Regime 1). When applied indiscriminately during Regime 2, the probability of a false breakout spikes dramatically. The elevated variance triggers the channel resistance threshold, executing the trade, but the dominant negative drift immediately pulls the asset back into the channel, triggering the strategy's tight ATR trailing stop and resulting in a realized loss.Advancing to the Causal Wasserstein HMMWhile classical HMMs are powerful explanatory tools, they suffer from critical operational vulnerabilities when applied to daily, rolling portfolio construction. Specifically, repeatedly re-estimating the model on expanding data windows can lead to the phenomenon of "label switching." In label switching, the numerical identities of the latent states randomly permute across estimation dates (e.g., State 1 abruptly becomes State 2), which destroys the stability of downstream strategy triggers and invalidates the economic interpretability of the model. This advanced model represents the market state at time $t$ via a continuous feature vector $x_t$ containing strictly historical, out-of-sample data. A highly effective formulation for this feature vector incorporates broad cross-asset dependencies to accurately gauge global liquidity and risk sentiment:$$x_t = \begin{bmatrix} r_t \\ \sigma_t \\ m_t \end{bmatrix} \in \mathbb{R}^{3N}$$Here, $r_t$ represents the vector of daily log returns across the asset universe, $\sigma_t$ represents the 60-day rolling volatility per asset, and $m_t$ is the 20-day rolling mean return. The target universe ($N$) must span beyond equities to include the S&P 500 (SPX), a broad bond proxy (BOND), Gold (GOLD), Oil (OIL), and the U.S. Dollar (USD) to accurately capture macro regime shifts.Instead of relying on static state assignments, the Wasserstein HMM dynamically selects the regime count $K_t$ using a predictive log-scoring criterion (PredLL) computed strictly on a rolling validation slice $\mathcal{V}_t$:$$K_t = \arg\max_{K \in [K_{\min}, K_{\max}]} \left\{ \sum_{s \in \mathcal{V}_t} \log p(x_s \mid \mathcal{F}_{s-1}, K) - \lambda_K \text{Complexity}(K) \right\}$$Crucially, to maintain regime identity across rolling windows without falling victim to label switching, the algorithm computes the 2-Wasserstein distance between the newly estimated multivariate Gaussian components $\mathcal{N}(\mu_{t,k}, \Sigma_{t,k})$ and a set of persistent, historical regime templates. Grounded in the foundational market microstructure research of Cont, Kukanov, and Stoikov (2014), OFI quantifies the net difference between supply and demand strictly within the electronic limit order book (LOB).The Cont-Kukanov-Stoikov framework operates on the empirically validated principle that mid-price changes over short horizons are linearly related to the imbalance of order book events at the best bid and ask. Let $V_t^B$ and $V_t^A$ represent the visible volume resting at the best bid and best ask, respectively, and let $P_t^B$ and $P_t^A$ represent their corresponding quoted prices. The order flow imbalance $e_t$ for any given discrete limit order book update is computed via the following precise formulation:$$e_t = I_{\{P_t^B \ge P_{t-1}^B\}} V_t^B - I_{\{P_t^B \le P_{t-1}^B\}} V_{t-1}^B - I_{\{P_t^A \le P_{t-1}^A\}} V_t^A + I_{\{P_t^A \ge P_{t-1}^A\}} V_{t-1}^A$$This equation dynamically and elegantly captures the addition of new limit orders, the cancellation of existing limit orders, and the consumption of resting liquidity by aggressive market orders. Over a defined analytical window, such as a 15-minute bar, the cumulative OFI provides a definitive, quantifiable metric of directional market pressure:$$OFI = \sum_{t=1}^N e_t$$Filtering Noise and Multi-Level Deep Book AnalysisWhile top-of-book OFI is powerful, it is susceptible to the noise generated by High-Frequency Trading (HFT) algorithms. The mathematical foundation for modern algorithmic execution is the Almgren-Chriss (2000) framework. This model elegantly mathematically formalizes the intrinsic trade-off between the market impact generated by rapid execution and the volatility (timing risk) incurred by slow execution.In the standard Almgren-Chriss formulation, an algorithmic trader is tasked with liquidating or acquiring a block of $X$ shares over a defined time horizon $T$. The execution trajectory is defined by the trading rate $v_t$. The underlying market price $S_t$ is modeled as following an arithmetic Brownian motion, which is directly modified by the trader's actions:$$dS_t = \sigma dW_t + g(v_t) dt$$where $\sigma dW_t$ represents the exogenous market volatility and $g(v_t)$ represents the permanent market impact—the long-term equilibrium shift in the asset's price caused by the information content of the trade itself.The actual execution price $\tilde{S}_t$ realized by the algorithmic trader is further degraded by temporary market impact, $h(v_t)$. This represents the instantaneous liquidity cost of demanding immediacy, such as crossing the bid-ask spread and depleting the first few resting levels of the limit order book. Assuming linear impact functions for mathematical tractability, $g(v_t) = \gamma v_t$ and $h(v_t) = \eta v_t$.The Implementation Shortfall (IS)—defined as the total cost of trading relative to the theoretical decision price $S_0$ at the exact moment the signal was generated—is given by:$$IS = X S_0 - \int_0^T v_t \tilde{S}_t dt$$Because the asset price is inherently stochastic, the Implementation Shortfall is a random variable. The objective of the optimal execution algorithm is to minimize a mean-variance utility function:$$\min_{v_t} \left( \mathbb{E}[IS] + \lambda \mathbb{V}ar[IS] \right)$$where the parameter $\lambda$ represents the specific risk aversion of the trader.Solving this constrained optimization problem via the Euler-Lagrange equations yields an optimal trading trajectory that strictly follows a hyperbolic sine function. The optimal number of shares $x_t$ to hold at any given time $t$ is:$$x_t = X \frac{\sinh(\kappa(T-t))}{\sinh(\kappa T)}$$where the curvature parameter $\kappa = \sqrt{\frac{\lambda \sigma^2}{\eta}}$ dictates how aggressively the algorithm front-loads the execution.Transitioning from Naive Benchmarks to Implementation Shortfall (IS) RoutingThe current operational protocol of filling passively at the "15m N+1 mid" is a uniform execution assumption that completely ignores the convexity of market impact.
